How to read this page

Each row of the first table is one figure for the whole network: how many channels and ties it has, how dense, reciprocal and clustered it is, how far apart its channels are, whether it holds together in one piece, and how unequally citations are spread. A statistic's name carries its definition as a tooltip.

Density is the share of the possible ties that exist; reciprocity the share of ties that are returned; clustering and transitivity say how often two channels tied to the same channel are tied to each other. Path lengths and the diameter count steps between channels inside the largest connected part of the network († ignoring the ties' direction, ‡ following it). A centralisation runs from 0, when the measure is spread evenly, to 1, when one channel holds it all.

Channels that are only cited, without being observed themselves, are left out of the statistics that need a channel's own citations: reciprocity, clustering, transitivity and the degree correlations.

Modularity by method says how much more the ties stay inside the communities a method found than chance would give. A high value alone proves little, because sparse random networks reach high values too: z and p compare it with random networks in which every channel cites and is cited as much as it really does.

Homophily by label group asks whether citations stay inside the analyst's labels more than the labels' sizes predict, each figure tested against random reshuffles of the labels. The dyadic regression weighs several explanations of who cites whom together. Where baseline columns are shown, z is the distance of the observed value from random networks, in their standard deviations.

With a timeline, the small bars beside a value show the same statistic period by period; the bar of the period on show is highlighted.

Degree distribution

How many channels fall in each band of in-strength or out-strength: the summed weight of the ties a channel receives or sends, its weighted degree.

Measure comparison

One dot per channel on two measures of its position, both axes on a logarithmic scale; the dashed line is the power-law trend.
